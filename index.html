Stackelberg game: the leader commits first, the follower best-responds, and the leader plans for that response.

The leader gets a first-mover advantage, so who leads is itself a policy choice. The authors suggest flipping leadership once AI capability crosses a threshold.

One product, two builders: a base-model lab sets up base camp, a specialist climbs to market.

Backfire: the lab free-rides on the rule, so final safety ends up lower than with no rule.

Rules on both: a commitment device. Safer, and both earn more.

Proved for every quadratic-cost game where the market already buys some safety. Backfire only happens with weak rules aimed at the downstream player.

WHAT THE PAPER DOESN'T SHOW

The "human" in the LLM test was also an LLM. Real people are untested.
It assumes a cooperative agent, not a deceptive one trying to hide.
The guarantees are design specs: they hold only if the designer sets the rewards right.
Only two players. With n agents, the authors can only conjecture it still works.

ZOOMING BACK OUT

I · THE RACE
Nobody slows down alone. Good outcomes needed coordination and verification.
II · THE GAME
Aligned agents can still reach a terrible equilibrium.
III · THE RULES
Who moves first changes the outcome, so leadership is a design choice.
IV · THE BACKFIRE
A rule changes everyone's incentives, sometimes in the wrong direction.
V · THE GUARDS
Attention is scarce: randomize it and aim it at expected harm.
VI · THE PAIR
Shape the reward so that autonomy and safety point the same way.

Safety is a property of the game, not just of the player.

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN 100 AIs SHARE ONE HUMAN?

Overman & Bayati conjecture their guarantees survive with many agents. Nobody has proved it yet.
